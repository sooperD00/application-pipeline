API key belongs to one workspace, and workspaces can carry lower spend limits than the parent organization. Put the public app's key in its own workspace with its own smaller limit, and a runaway funnel can't eat the credits you need for your own job search or the schema-extraction runs. The public thing fails; your thing keeps working.

The instinct is sound but there's an arithmetic problem specific to that number: card processing is roughly 2.9% plus a flat 30¢, and that flat 30¢ is murder on a $2 charge — you'd lose something like 18% off the top before a single token gets spent. Micro-transactions are where fixed per-transaction fees do the most damage. If the goal is genuinely "cover our costs," a $2 price point covers them noticeably less well than it looks on paper, and you'd want to check whether Stripe's micropayment rate is available to you before assuming otherwise.

The bigger thing, though, is that the friction isn't the two dollars — it's the card form. Going from free to any price at all costs you far more than the price suggests, because the conversion cliff is the moment you ask a stranger for payment details, not the moment you name a number. And that's what makes me want to push on where you'd put the ask rather than whether. Your funnel is already designed anonymous-first: deliver value, then gate. The intake calls are the cheap ones and the 25-JD session is the expensive one, so the natural gate is exactly where your design already puts it — free intake and score, then pay to run a session. Charging at the door instead would invert the funnel you and your brother built.

Reshaped that way, a pack probably beats a single charge — some number of sessions for ten dollars, say — because the flat fee amortizes and you stop optimizing around a price that barely survives its own processing. Worth keeping the part of your instinct that's genuinely right, though: a small price is a real quality filter, and people who pay something give sharply better feedback than people who don't. It's also an honest story for a job-search tool, where "free" invites the reasonable suspicion that the user is the product.
